22Three Months, 724 Commits — The Git History

The commit log tells the real story of RealLearn: a hackathon sprint, a month of silence, and then a sustained, agent-driven crescendo that produced two thirds of the entire history in its final month.

The shape of the history

Between the first commit on 2026-05-06 and the most recent merge on 2026-07-26 — a span of 82 calendar days — the main branch accumulated 724 commits, 239 merged pull requests (the highest numbered being PR #263), and contributions from 9 distinct author identities. Work happened on only 41 of those 82 days, which means the effective pace on active days was roughly 17–18 commits per day. This is not a slow-burn open-source project; it is a burst-mode product effort, driven by one owner orchestrating a rotating cast of AI coding agents.

Four eras of work

Era 1 — The hackathon sprint (May 6–13)

The repository opens with 346944f Initial commit followed the same day by bbe3032 Initial plan and de09e6b Build complete RealLearn web application — both authored by copilot-swe-agent[bot]. The very first pull requests (#1 create-reallearn-web-app, #2 make-ui-world-class, #3 fix-critical-mobile-rendering-bugs) were all agent branches. May 7 was the single busiest day in the entire history at 58 commits, and May 11 (42 commits) closed the sprint with google-labs-jules[bot] landing f5c6d8f Migrate to Gemma 4 and implement progressive unlock logic and the telling subject line e4e1a2b Finalize hackathon project: Gemma 4 migration, progressive unlock, and exhaustive quiz explanations. The product's two defining mechanics — Gemma-generated lessons and the quiz-gated progressive unlock — were in place within the first week.

Era 2 — The silence (May 18 – June 20)

After a trickle of fixes through May 18, the log goes completely quiet for 32 days. This gap matters for interpreting everything that follows: the project's own changelog (change-made-after-submission.md) frames the hackathon deliverable as "a working prototype with a single AI provider, no authentication, no legal framework," and the post-gap history is explicitly a campaign to close that distance.

Era 3 — The return (June 20–30)

The comeback begins with a design pivot — e55b098 Redesign: dark gold-noir → classic printed-textbook aesthetic (June 20) — and then compresses 100 commits into the last three days of June. June 28–30 alone (95 commits) delivered chat persistence and the sidebar, Clerk account integration, consent records in MongoDB, the toast system, skip-to-content accessibility work, the gamification system (XP, streaks, achievements), the fail-open LLM moderation layer, and the quiz-shuffle verification harness. This era also contains the first revert pair in the log (Revert "Agent/Rapid Spark 5nqx", June 30) — an early sign of the agent-supervision pattern discussed below.

Era 4 — The July crescendo (July 1–30)

July's 461 commits contain the provider-migration endgame (Cerebras primary with Cloudflare fallback, July 13), three major security sweeps (July 5, 6, and 22), the legal-version ratchet (Privacy Policy v1.2 through v2.7), the creation of the agent-governance layer (docs/AGENT_MEMORY.md, July 20), and an extraordinary sequence of full UI redesigns — liquid glass, Japanese, Indian tricolor, Still Ink, Soft Pastel, Gen Z, and finally Cyber Aqua — covered in Chapter 23. The run closed on July 26–30: July 26 saw 33 commits and ten merged PRs (#254–#263), ending with 6ebd48a fix(theme): enhance AI response text contrast and readability in lite mode, and July 30 merged PR #280, the Cyber Aqua redesign that defines the current working tree.

The agent-driven character shows up beyond authorship. And the documentation itself bears agent fingerprints: both AGENT_MEMORY.md and change-made-after-submission.md currently contain leftover merge-conflict residue (stray fix/lite-mode-ai-response-readability and main branch-label lines from a July 26 conflict resolution) — a small but honest artifact of high-velocity, multi-agent editing.

The rhythm of the busiest days

The ten heaviest commit days account for 360 commits — half the entire history — and each maps cleanly to a named campaign. May 7 (58 commits) was raw scaffolding, the day after the initial agent build. May 11 (42) was hackathon finalization. June 28 and 29 (37 and 39) carried the legal-and-consent framework, the footer and accessibility passes, and the domain move to reallearn.site. July 4 (29) was the truncation-validation incident and its fixes. July 13 (32) was the Cerebras migration and performance overhaul. July 19 (35) was the Japanese redesign. July 25 (27) and July 26 (33) were the Gen Z pivot and the five-redesign finale. The pattern is consistent: this history does not accrete gradually — it advances in named, single-theme offensives, usually one agent session long, each closed out with documentation updates the same day. Between offensives the log drops to zero, not to a trickle; there is no background hum of maintenance commits, because there is no team to hum. The two revert commits and the lone debug: commit cluster exactly where one would predict — at the end of June, when an agent branch (agent/rapid-spark-5nqx) had to be rolled back wholesale, the clearest single data point on the supervision costs of the model.

PR cadence and milestones

All 239 merged PRs belong to a single-owner review model: agents (or the owner's second identity) open branches, and "AL-Aqmar tinwala" merges them. The cadence accelerated sharply — PRs #1–#3 on day one, #25 by May 11, then a long plateau, and finally a run to #263 with roughly 65 PRs merged in the last week of July alone. Merge commits total 257 (239 PR merges plus 18 back-merges of main into feature branches), so about one in three commits on main is process overhead — the cost of routing every change, however small, through a branch-and-PR loop as the Change Protocol requires.

Reading the log honestly

The 2:1 ratio of fix to feat commits, three full-codebase bug sweeps, and 18 documented redesign passes are the signature of an agent-velocity codebase: features arrive fast and cheaply, and a disproportionate share of subsequent effort goes to correcting, hardening, and re-styling what the agents produced. The project's own governance response — the AGENT_MEMORY.md protocol, "de-slop" rules, and mandatory verification commands — is itself one of the most interesting artifacts in the history: process written for machines, by their operator, in response to observed failure modes.
